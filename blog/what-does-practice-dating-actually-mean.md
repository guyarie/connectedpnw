# What Does “Practice Dating” Actually Mean?

Dating is one of those things we're somehow expected to know how to do.

There isn't much opportunity to practice. There usually isn't useful feedback. And when something doesn't go well, we often don't know why.

Maybe you can get first dates, but they rarely turn into second or third dates. Maybe you haven't dated much at all and feel unsure about where to begin. Or maybe you've been dating for years but keep finding yourself in familiar situations that don't lead where you want them to.

The usual advice is to just keep putting yourself out there.

But what if dating could include something else?

What if there were actually room to **practice?**

## Practice Doesn't Mean Pretending

When we talk about "practice dating" at Connected, we don't mean role-playing a date or rehearsing the right things to say.

The interactions are real.

You're spending time with another person. You're having conversations. You're deciding what you'd like to do together. You may feel comfortable, awkward, interested, uncertain, attracted, disappointed—or several of those things at once.

That's precisely what makes the experience useful.

The difference is the **context**.

Both people know they're there to learn. Neither person has to pretend that every interaction is effortless. There is room to notice what's happening, talk about it, receive feedback, and try something different.

## Think About How We Learn Almost Anything Else

If you were learning to dance, nobody would expect you to understand everything after being shown the steps once.

You'd try them.

You'd probably get some things wrong.

Someone might help you notice what you're doing. You'd adjust. You'd try again.

Dating rarely gives us that opportunity.

You might go on a date, think it went well, and then receive a text saying the other person "didn't feel a connection." Or you might know that something felt off but have no idea what happened.

There usually isn't a conversation afterward that helps either person understand the experience.

So we fill in the blanks ourselves.

*I talked too much.*

*I wasn't interesting enough.*

*There wasn't enough chemistry.*

*I'm terrible at dating.*

Sometimes those explanations are accurate. Often, we simply don't know.

Practice gives us an opportunity to replace some of that guessing with **curiosity and feedback**.

## What Are You Actually Practicing?

There isn't one correct way to date, and Connected isn't designed to teach people a dating script.

Instead, practice might involve noticing things like:

How do I show someone I'm interested?

What happens when I'm nervous?

Do I ask questions because I'm genuinely curious, or because I'm trying to keep the conversation going?

Can I let someone know me, or do I stay in the safer role of asking about them?

Can I say what I want?

Can I hear what the other person wants—even when it's different from what I hoped to hear?

What happens when there's an awkward moment?

Can I give someone feedback kindly?

Can I receive feedback without immediately deciding I've done something wrong?

And perhaps one of the biggest questions:

**Am I so focused on whether this person likes me that I forget to notice whether I like them?**

These aren't tricks for getting someone to choose you. They're opportunities to learn more about how **you participate in connection**.

## And You're Practicing With Another Person

This part matters.

Connection isn't an individual performance.

Two people create an interaction together. Each brings their own preferences, history, communication style, hopes, uncertainties and ways of relating.

That's why practice isn't only about developing confidence or becoming "better at dating."

It's also about **mutuality**.

Can you notice the other person while staying connected to yourself?

Can you make room for their needs without abandoning your own?

Can the two of you talk about what is happening between you rather than each privately trying to figure it out?

Sometimes the learning comes from something going well.

Sometimes it comes from something being uncomfortable.

Both can be valuable.

## What Practice Looks Like at Connected

Connected begins with workshops and social experiences where participants get to know one another while exploring some of these ideas together.

Then participants have a series of initial meetings with potential practice partners.

These aren't traditional dates, and no one is assigned a partner without having a say in the decision. Participants have input into whom they would like to continue practicing with.

Once paired, partners spend approximately three months practicing together, supported by weekly coaching.

The pair decides what they want to practice between sessions. That might mean meeting for coffee, planning an activity, having dinner, navigating a more vulnerable conversation, or experimenting with something that represents a later stage of dating.

Then you have somewhere to bring what happened.

**What did you notice? What felt easy? What felt difficult? What would you like to try differently next time?**

Over time, the practice can evolve rather than repeatedly starting over with another first date.

## The Goal Isn't to Get It Right

Guided practice doesn't eliminate trial and error.

It actually makes more room for it.

You might try something and discover it doesn't work for you. You might receive feedback that surprises you. You may realize that something you've thought of as a weakness isn't actually a problem—or notice a pattern you hadn't been able to see before.

The goal isn't perfect dating.

And Connected doesn't promise that every interaction will go well or that you'll find a romantic partner through the program.

The goal is to have a place where mistakes don't automatically mean failure.

They can become **information**.

## Dating Is Already Practice. We Just Usually Do It Without Support.

Every date you've ever been on has taught you something, whether you realized it at the time or not.

Connected makes that learning more intentional.

You still have to take risks.

You still won't know exactly what another person is thinking.

You may still experience awkwardness, attraction, uncertainty, disappointment and excitement.

In other words, it still resembles real dating.

But instead of being left alone to figure out what happened, you have a structure for slowing down, getting feedback, experimenting and trying again.

**Learn together. Practice with support. Stay connected.**

That's what we mean by practice dating.
